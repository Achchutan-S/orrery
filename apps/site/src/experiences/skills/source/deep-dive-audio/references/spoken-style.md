# Writing for the Ear

The listener can't scroll back, skim, or glance at a heading. Everything has to land the first time it's heard. These notes explain how to write so it does.

## Sentences and rhythm

Short and medium sentences, varied. A long sentence with three clauses is fine on a page but loses a listener halfway through. If a sentence needs a parenthetical, split it into two sentences instead.

Lead with the point, then the detail. "Kafka keeps every message for days. That's the surprising part." works better aloud than burying the surprise at the end of a long clause.

Use punctuation for pauses. Full stops and commas create natural breathing room for a TTS voice. Dashes work for a beat of emphasis. Colons are fine. Semicolons read as a flat pause and are usually better as a full stop.

## Sounding like a person, not a textbook

Compare:

> Bauhaus emerged as a reaction against excessive ornamentation and emphasized functionalist principles.

> Now, this is where things start to change. Bauhaus basically asks a simple question: what if a building or a chair didn't need decoration just for the sake of it? What if its shape came from what it actually had to do?

The second one is a person thinking out loud with you. That's the voice. Use "you" freely. Ask the question the listener is probably wondering, then answer it. Occasional plain-spoken words like "basically", "here's the thing", "and that's the catch" are welcome in moderation — they're how people actually explain things.

## Jargon

When a technical term is needed:

1. Say the term.
2. Explain it in plain English immediately.
3. Give a quick example if it helps.
4. Use it naturally from then on.

> An API is basically a way for two pieces of software to talk to each other. Your app asks the server for something through the API, and the server sends the answer back.

Don't assume specialist vocabulary unless the topic clearly requires it, and even then, a one-line refresher costs little.

## Analogies and mental pictures

Give big ideas a picture the listener can hold:

> Think of TCP like sending a registered parcel. You don't just drop it in the post and hope. You care whether it arrived, whether pieces are missing, and whether they came in the right order.

Good analogies map the important structure, not just the surface. After using one, it's often worth saying where it breaks down ("unlike a parcel, though, TCP will resend the missing piece automatically"). Never use an analogy that leaves a wrong idea behind just because it sounds simple.

## Transitions

Spoken explanations need explicit glue, because the listener can't see the structure. Connect each part to the last:

- "Once you've got that, the next piece falls into place."
- "So that solves one problem. But it creates another."
- "Now, these two sound similar, but there's an important difference."
- "Which brings us back to the question we started with."

Avoid announcing what you're about to do in a generic way. "Now let's explore the next fascinating concept" says nothing; a transition that carries meaning does the same job better.

## Comparisons

When two things get confused, compare them out loud rather than in a table:

> Both are ways to store data, but they make opposite bets. A relational database bets that your data has a clear shape you know in advance. A document store bets that it doesn't.

## Fact, opinion, dispute

Make the status of a claim audible:

- Established: just say it.
- Contested: "Historians still argue about this. One camp says… the other says…"
- Prediction or opinion: "Supporters think… critics worry that…"
- Uncertain: "The numbers here are rough, but most estimates put it around…"

## Numbers, dates and symbols

TTS engines handle some of these badly, so write them the way you'd say them:

- Years: "twenty twenty-six", "nineteen forty-five", "the eleventh century".
- Percentages: "five percent", not "5%".
- Big numbers: "about three hundred million", not "~300,000,000". Round when precision doesn't matter to understanding.
- Currency: "two billion dollars", "fifty crore rupees".
- Units and ranges: "ten to fifteen milliseconds", not "10–15ms".
- Avoid "~", "&", "e.g.", "i.e.", "etc.", "vs." — say "about", "and", "for example", "that is", "and so on", "versus".
- Avoid slash words like "and/or", "input/output", "client/server" — say "input and output", "client and server".

## Abbreviations

Spell an abbreviation out the first time, then use the short form: "Application Programming Interface, or API." Where a TTS voice might mispronounce an acronym, a quick hint helps the first time, for example "SQL, which most people pronounce sequel".

## Phrases to avoid

These make it sound like generic AI writing. Use them only if one genuinely fits:

- "It's important to note that…"
- "Let's dive into…" / "Let's unpack this…"
- "In today's rapidly evolving world…"
- "As we explore…"
- "Whether you're a beginner or an expert…"
- "In conclusion…"
- Endings like "I hope this was helpful!" or "Let me know if you have any questions!"

## Repetition that helps

Listeners can't reread, so returning to a key idea at a turning point is useful: "Remember the parcel analogy? This is exactly the case where a piece goes missing." That's different from padding, which repeats without adding. Repeat to reconnect; never to fill time.

## Endings

Close with "Before you move on, remember these few things." and three to seven points that compress the topic into a mental model someone could explain to a friend. Each point should be a sentence or two, phrased to stick. Then one or two sentences that land the idea — a "so what" or a thought to carry away. Stop there.
