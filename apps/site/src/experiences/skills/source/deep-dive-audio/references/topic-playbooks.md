# Topic Playbooks

Different subjects tell their story differently. Pick the arc that fits, rename the parts in natural language, merge or drop parts that would be filler, and blend arcs when a topic straddles types (a company built on a technology, a scientific discovery with a dramatic history). These are starting shapes, not forms.

## Technology, tool or system
The listener wants to know why it exists and how it works inside.
1. The problem it solves, and what people did before it.
2. The core idea in one sentence and one picture.
3. How it works, piece by piece: core components, then how they fit together.
4. A walk-through example: follow one request, message or file through the system.
5. Trade-offs: what it's great at, what it's bad at, what it costs.
6. Where it's used in the real world, and what it's commonly confused with.
7. How it evolved and where it's heading.

## Historical event or era
The listener wants cause and consequence, not a list of dates.
1. The world before: what was normal, what was under strain.
2. The causes: long-term pressures and the immediate spark.
3. What happened, told as a sequence with a few key people at the centre.
4. Turning points: the moments where it could have gone differently.
5. Consequences, short-term and long-term.
6. Why it still matters and how people interpret it today, including where historians disagree.

## Company or organisation
The listener wants to understand how it actually makes money and why it wins or struggles.
1. Origins: who started it, and the problem or bet behind it.
2. The business model in plain terms: who pays, for what, and why.
3. Products and how the company really operates day to day.
4. Its edge: technology, scale, brand, network effects, or whatever genuinely matters.
5. Competition and industry position.
6. The economics: rough scale, margins, what drives growth. Check current figures.
7. Current situation and the challenges ahead. Separate facts from analyst opinion.

## Scientific concept or discovery
The listener wants intuition first, then mechanism, then evidence.
1. An everyday hook or puzzle that the idea explains.
2. The basic idea in plain words.
3. The mechanism: how it actually works.
4. A concrete example or thought experiment.
5. The evidence: how we know, and who found it out.
6. Applications.
7. Limitations, open questions and common misconceptions.

## Broad field or discipline
The listener wants a map they can navigate later.
1. What the field studies and the central question it keeps asking.
2. How it grew: the founding ideas and big shifts.
3. The main branches or schools and how they differ.
4. The handful of ideas everyone in the field knows.
5. What practitioners actually do.
6. Current frontiers and debates.
7. How to keep exploring: what to learn next.

## Cultural, artistic or philosophical topic
The listener wants to see the ideas in motion and in examples.
1. Origins and the context it reacted against.
2. The core ideas or aesthetic, made concrete with examples.
3. Major movements, people and works.
4. How the different strands differ from each other.
5. Influence and modern interpretations.

## Current trend or debate
The listener wants to understand the forces and the sides fairly.
1. What's happening and why it's in the news now. Search for the latest.
2. Background needed to make sense of it.
3. The forces driving it.
4. The main positions, each given its strongest case.
5. What the evidence actually says, and where it's thin.
6. What to watch for next.

## Person
The listener wants to know why this person mattered.
1. The world they entered and what shaped them.
2. The key decisions and work, as a story.
3. What they changed.
4. Controversies and criticism, fairly stated.
5. Their legacy.

## A "how does X work" or "X versus Y" question
Keep it tighter. Open with the puzzle, build the one or two foundations needed, walk through the mechanism or the comparison with a concrete example, then close with the mental model. Skip history unless it explains the design.
